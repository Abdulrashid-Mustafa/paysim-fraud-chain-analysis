Fraudsters don't steal money once. They move it twice.

Transfer in. Cash out. Gone within the hour.

I spent 11 years investigating social engineering disputes before I moved into analytics, and the pattern was always the same: by the time a case reached my desk, the money had already left. So I went looking for the point where it could still be stopped.

Using PaySim, a public dataset of 6.36M simulated mobile money transactions, I mapped the full transfer to cash out chain.

Four things came out of it.

1. The money is gone almost immediately.
99.2% of fraud chains completed the cash out in the SAME HOUR as the transfer. None took longer than one hour. Any control that waits for a human to open a review queue is already too late.

2. $6.07B was targeted. 98.7% of it got out.
Once the transfer clears, liquidation succeeds almost every time. The interception window is the transfer leg, not the cash out.

3. The existing control caught 16 cases out of 8,213.
That is 0.19% recall. It flags single transfers above a fixed amount, so it caught nothing at all on the cash out leg. A static threshold on one leg of a two leg chain cannot see the chain.

4. My best signal failed, and that was the most useful result.
Account draining looked decisive: 97.6% of fraud fully empties the origin account. Then I measured the false positives. 42.7% of LEGITIMATE transactions do the same thing, because emptying a mobile wallet is normal behaviour.

As a standalone rule it would generate 148 alerts for every fraud case caught. No operations team survives that.

Combining draining with the high value band cut alert volume by 94% and raised precision sixfold, at the cost of recall. That trade off is the real deliverable.

The lesson I keep coming back to: a rule reported with recall but without alert volume is not a recommendation. It is a demo. Anyone who has staffed a fraud queue knows the difference.
